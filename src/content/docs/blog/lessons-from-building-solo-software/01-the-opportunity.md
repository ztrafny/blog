I built a software tool that a team of about 50 people uses every day to do their jobs. I am not a software engineer. I have no CS degree, no formal engineering training. I've spent the last decade in product management, most recently managing a team at Amazon. And yet here I am, a single person, building and maintaining a production tool that a real company depends on.

I don't say this to brag. I say it because I think it signals something much bigger than my own project.

For most of software's history, building anything real required a team. Engineers, designers, product managers, QA — the overhead was significant, and for good reason. Software is complex. But that overhead meant there was a threshold: the problem you were solving had to be big enough to justify the cost of solving it. Countless niche problems — the kind that affect a team of 50, not a market of 50,000 — simply weren't worth the investment. They went unsolved. People used spreadsheets, or workarounds, or just accepted the inefficiency.

That threshold is collapsing.

A single person with technical fluency and a deep understanding of the problem space can now build tools that would have required a team of five or ten just a few years ago. The cost of going from idea to working software has dropped by an order of magnitude, and that changes the math on what's worth building. Markets that were too small before suddenly aren't. Problems that couldn't justify a development team can now justify one person with the right skills and the right partnership.

And this isn't theoretical. I'm living it. The tool I built — Loan Decoder — serves a company called Student Loan Tutor LLC. Their team uses it daily to navigate the labyrinth of federal student loan policy. It's not a side project or a prototype. It's infrastructure. And one person built it.

What excites me about this shift isn't just my own story. It's the implications. More people from non-traditional backgrounds are going to build things. More small, sharp, purpose-built tools are going to exist for problems that previously went unaddressed. More domain experts are going to look at the inefficiencies in their own fields and realize: I could actually solve this now. The barrier to entry hasn't disappeared, but it has moved. And when you lower the cost of creation, you don't just get cheaper versions of what already exists — you get entirely new categories of things that never existed before.

I think we're early in understanding what this means. The conversation tends to focus on the tools and the technology, but the real story is economic. When building gets cheaper, more gets built. When more gets built, more people's specific needs get met. That's a good thing.

This is the first post in a three-part series about what I've learned building Loan Decoder. Next, I'll dig into the most critical — and most overlooked — factor in making something like this work: the partnership with the person who actually understands the domain.
