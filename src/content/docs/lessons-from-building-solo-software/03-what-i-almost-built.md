I came into this project with what I thought was an advantage: years of domain familiarity. I'd worked in finance-adjacent product roles. I had opinions. I'd thought a lot about what good software in a complex policy domain looks like, and I had a clear vision for what Loan Decoder should be.

Most of that vision was wrong.

Not subtly wrong. Wrong in the way that, if I had built what I'd planned to build, my partner would have looked at it, said some version of "this isn't what we need," and we would have parted ways. The product I'd designed in my head was a confident, comprehensive answering machine: ask it any question about a borrower's situation, get back the optimal repayment strategy, complete with reasoning. Smart. Self-contained. The kind of thing a product manager pitches in a deck.

What they actually needed was much more grounded. Tools that made their existing process faster and more consistent. Reliable lookups. Faster intake. A way to capture the reasoning that an experienced specialist already does in their head, so a less experienced specialist could do the same work without missing things. The kind of thing that doesn't pitch well in a deck but actually changes what someone's day looks like.

I had to be told this. More than once. There's a particular kind of resistance that comes up when you're certain you know the right answer and the person across the table is telling you, politely, that you don't. It feels like they don't get the vision. (Or at least, it feels that way for the first few rounds.) Eventually it dawned on me that the vision was strong but disconnected. It described a tool that someone might want to look at in a demo, not one anyone would use day to day.

This is a familiar lesson in product. "You are not your customer." It's a thing every PM has heard, and probably said. What surprised me was how much harder it was to apply when I thought I had domain knowledge. Domain familiarity, I think, is sneakier than ignorance. Ignorance knows it doesn't know. Familiarity convinces you that you do, and then you stop listening at exactly the moment you most need to.

The shift happened slowly. It looked like letting their actual workflow lead the design, instead of my mental model of what the workflow should be. Which, in practice, meant a lot of listening and a lot of editing my ideas before voicing them. I'd notice myself about to say "what about this great feature" and stop, because the question of what they needed was a lot more interesting once I treated it as something I had to learn instead of something I already knew.

I think there's a strategic point in here that's bigger than the design lesson. In a market this small, building from what the customer actually does is how the product survives. There's no slack the way there is in consumer software, where you can ship something half-right and let usage data sort it out. If you build the wrong thing for fifty people, fifty people stop using it, and your project is over. The most direct path to a tool that survives in a small market is to start from what the customer actually does, and to keep checking back.

I came in confident I understood the domain. By now I'd describe what I have differently: a working sense of where my instincts are likely to be off, and a habit of asking before assuming.
