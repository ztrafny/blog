I built a software tool that a team of about 50 people uses every day to do their jobs. I am not a software engineer. I have no CS degree, no formal engineering training. I've spent the last decade in product management, most recently managing a team at Amazon. And yet here I am, a single person, building and maintaining a production tool that a real company depends on.

It points to something much bigger than my own project.

For most of software's history, building anything real required a team. Engineers, designers, product managers, QA. The overhead was significant, and for good reason. Software is complex. But that overhead meant there was a threshold: the problem you were solving had to be big enough to justify the cost of solving it. Countless niche problems (the kind that affect a team of 50, not a market of 50,000) simply weren't worth the investment. They went unsolved. People used spreadsheets, or workarounds, or just accepted the inefficiency.

That threshold is collapsing.

A single person with technical fluency and a deep understanding of the problem space can now build tools that would have required a team of five or ten just a few years ago. The cost of going from idea to working software has dropped by an order of magnitude, and that changes the math on what's worth building. Markets that were too small before suddenly aren't. Problems that couldn't justify a development team can now justify one person who knows how to build, paired with someone who knows the problem cold.

The tool I built, Loan Decoder, serves a company called Student Loan Tutor LLC. Their team uses it daily to make sense of federal student loan policy. The whole business runs on it, and one person built it.

What excites me about this isn't my own story. It's what it implies for everyone else. People from non-traditional backgrounds are going to build things they couldn't build before, and not just clones of what already exists. Domain experts are going to look at the inefficiencies in their own fields and realize they could actually solve them now. The barrier to entry hasn't disappeared, but it has moved. And when you lower the cost of creation, you don't just get cheaper versions of what's already here. You get whole categories of things that didn't exist before.

I think we're early in understanding what this means. The conversation tends to focus on the tools and the technology, but the real story is economic, and slower-moving than the tech-focused version suggests. When the cost of building drops, the math changes for what's worth building. More software is going to get made, sure. The interesting question is who's making it, and for whom.
